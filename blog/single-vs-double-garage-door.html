When a garage door reaches the end of its life, or when you are finishing a new build, one of the first decisions is whether to go single or double. For most San Diego homeowners, the answer seems obvious until they start thinking about driveway width, HOA rules, curb appeal, and what it costs to keep each type running over time. This guide walks through the real differences so you can make the right call for your specific home and neighborhood.

I install and repair both types across all of San Diego County, from the narrower lots of North Park and Mission Hills to the wide suburban driveways in Poway, Rancho Bernardo, and Chula Vista. What works well on one property is often the wrong choice on another, and the gap between the two options is bigger than most people expect.

Single-Car Garage Doors: Sizes, Pros, and Cons

A standard single-car door is 8 to 10 feet wide, with 9 by 7 feet being the most common size in San Diego homes built before the 2000s. Eight-foot doors show up in older bungalows and smaller condos; 10-foot doors appear where the builder wanted extra clearance for larger vehicles. Height is almost always 7 feet, though 8-foot options are available if you drive a lifted truck or a cargo van.

Single doors fit one standard vehicle, which covers the majority of daily drivers. They are lighter, use a smaller motor, and are generally less expensive to buy and operate. When something does fail, say a spring or cable, the repair cost is lower than on a double door because the parts are smaller.

  • Lower installed cost: Single-car doors sit at the lower end of the new-door price range.
  • Easier on the opener: A lighter door puts less strain on the motor, which can extend opener life.
  • Works in narrow openings: Many older San Diego homes were built with a single opening and retrofitting a double is a structural project, not just a hardware swap.
  • Two independent systems: If you have two single doors side by side, a failure on one does not affect the other bay.

The main drawback is clearance. A 9-foot-wide door is tight for a full-size truck or SUV, and backing in can feel uncomfortable. If your household has grown to two vehicles or you use the garage as a workshop, a single door can make the space feel cramped.

Double-Car Garage Doors: Sizes, Pros, and Cons

A standard double-car door is 16 feet wide and 7 feet tall. Some newer homes in communities like Scripps Ranch or San Marcos have 18-foot openings, which gives a little extra room on each side. Eight-foot heights are available here too, and are worth the upgrade if you park a large SUV or work van.

A double door covers the entire two-car opening with one panel. That makes it look cleaner on the front of the house, and it is often the right proportional choice for homes with a wider facade. Many newer garage door installations in San Diego go double for this reason alone.

  • Cleaner curb appeal: One large door often looks more proportional on a wider garage than two separate doors.
  • Easy two-vehicle access: No center post means wide-open access, which is useful for larger vehicles and maneuvering in a tight driveway.
  • Single opener: One motor and one set of controls covers both bays, which simplifies programming and remotes.

The tradeoff is that a double door is one system. A spring failure, a cable issue, or an opener problem takes both bays offline at once. Repair costs are also higher because a double door typically uses a pair of torsion springs, and the door itself is significantly heavier. For homeowners who depend on garage access every day, that single point of failure is worth weighing carefully. Keeping up with annual maintenance matters more for a double door than for two independent singles.

San Diego-Specific Factors That Affect the Choice

San Diego's climate and housing stock create a few considerations that do not come up in generic guides.

Coastal Salt Air

Homes within a mile of the coast, including La Jolla, Ocean Beach, Pacific Beach, Coronado, and Encinitas, deal with salt-laden air that accelerates corrosion on metal hardware. A double door has more hardware than two singles combined: a larger spring assembly, heavier cables, and wider tracks. More hardware means more surface area for corrosion to attack. This does not mean you should avoid a double door if you live near the water, but it does mean annual lubrication and hardware checks become more important. I use marine-grade grease on coastal jobs for this reason.

Lot Size and Driveway Width

Older San Diego neighborhoods were built on smaller lots with narrower driveways. In areas like North Park, University Heights, and Hillcrest, a 16-foot double door can be hard to back into without pulling wide into traffic. A pair of 8-foot or 9-foot single doors leaves a center post that breaks up the opening, but each door is easier to enter independently. If your driveway is less than 20 feet wide, I usually recommend measuring before committing to a double.

HOA Requirements

Many San Diego communities, particularly in planned developments in Scripps Ranch, Rancho Bernardo, Carmel Mountain Ranch, and Chula Vista, have rules about door style, panel design, and color. A double door's appearance has to match the overall facade and the HOA's design standards. Before ordering, check your CC&Rs so you do not end up with a door you cannot install or have to replace a second time.

Inland Heat

East County communities like El Cajon, Santee, Lakeside, and Alpine see summer temperatures that routinely hit the high 90s. Thermal expansion affects all door materials, but it is hardest on wood and composite. If you are drawn to a wood-look door, a steel door with a wood-grain emboss holds up to the heat better and will not warp. This applies equally to single and double doors, but the larger surface area of a double panel amplifies the effect.

Which Door Is Right for Your Situation?

There is no universal right answer, but the decision usually comes down to a few concrete factors. Here is a quick reference:

Choose a Single Door When...

Your opening is 8 to 10 feet wide, you park one vehicle, your driveway is narrow, or you want two independent systems for a two-car garage.

Choose a Double Door When...

Your opening is 16 feet or wider, you park two vehicles side by side, curb appeal on a wide facade matters, or you are doing a new installation on a newer home.

If your current opening is a single and you want a double, that conversion involves framing and structural work, not just a new door. I can handle the door, opener, and hardware side of things. The structural framing needs a general contractor first. On a straight swap, same size and style, I can usually complete the job the same day you call.

If you are replacing an existing door and are unsure whether the opening can handle a larger size, I can come out, measure, and give you a clear answer before anything is ordered. That free assessment saves a lot of back-and-forth and prevents surprises on install day.

Frequently Asked Questions

How wide is a standard single vs double garage door?

A standard single-car garage door is 8 to 10 feet wide, with 9 feet being the most common size in San Diego. A double-car door is typically 16 feet wide, though 18-foot openings appear in newer homes. Both are usually 7 feet tall, with 8-foot options available for taller trucks and SUVs.

Is a double garage door harder to repair than a single?

A double door uses one large panel rather than two separate panels, which means a single spring or cable failure takes both bays offline at once. Spring replacement typically costs more because a double door usually uses a pair of torsion springs. The repairs themselves are not more complicated, just slightly higher in cost due to the larger parts involved.

Can I convert a single-car garage opening to a double-car opening?

Yes, but it involves structural work beyond the garage door itself. The header beam above the single opening needs to be replaced with a wider beam to span the new double opening. A general contractor handles the structural framing; Noah handles the door, tracks, and hardware once the opening is ready.

Which door type works better near San Diego's coast?

Both types perform well with the right material and finish. Steel doors with a quality coating hold up better to salt air than bare wood or aluminum. Regardless of type, annual lubrication and hardware checks matter most for homes within a mile of the ocean in neighborhoods like La Jolla, Ocean Beach, or Coronado.
