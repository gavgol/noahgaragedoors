A garage door that opens only a few inches and then suddenly stops is one of the most disorienting problems you can have. The motor runs, the door begins to move, and then nothing. It reverses or freezes. For homeowners across San Diego County, from Mira Mesa to National City, this issue is more common than most people realize, and in most cases it has a clear, fixable cause.

The key is knowing which of the most common culprits is to blame. Some, like a low limit setting or a bit of debris in the track, you can address yourself in minutes. Others, like a broken torsion spring, require a technician because the repairs involve components under serious tension. This guide walks through every cause in order of likelihood so you can diagnose the problem before making a call.

Why Your Garage Door Stops After a Few Inches

The first thing to understand is that the stop behavior is intentional. Every garage door opener is designed to halt and reverse if it detects resistance, or a signal that the door has already reached its programmed travel height. That is a safety feature. The problem is that this protection can activate incorrectly when one of several components is out of spec.

Here are the five causes we encounter most often serving communities across San Diego County:

  • Broken torsion spring or detached cable
  • Open-limit setting configured too low
  • Obstruction in the door track or a broken roller
  • Opener force sensitivity set too high
  • Partial disconnect between the trolley arm and the door

Work through these in order. A weak or broken spring accounts for the majority of cases we see from Rancho Penasquitos to Chula Vista. Safety sensors are a common garage door problem, but they govern the closing cycle only, so they are not a cause of a door stopping while going up. If that's your symptom, skip straight to the spring section below.

Broken Springs and Failing Cables: The Most Common Cause

A residential garage door typically weighs between 100 and 250 pounds. The torsion spring mounted above the door is what counterbalances that weight. When the spring is intact, the garage door opener only needs a small push to move the door. When the spring breaks, the full weight of the door falls on the opener's motor.

The motor will try to lift this weight, manage an inch or two, and then the auto-force protection activates and stops everything. This is the safest possible outcome in a bad situation. Look above the door: if you see a visible gap in the spring coils, or if you heard a loud bang when the problem started, a broken torsion spring is almost certainly the cause.

Do not attempt to lift or operate the door manually when a spring is broken. The door can fall suddenly and cause serious injury. Spring replacement involves winding steel under high tension and requires the right tools and experience. We handle broken spring replacements the same day across San Diego County, and the job typically takes under an hour.

A broken or detached cable causes a nearly identical symptom. The cable runs from the bottom corner of the door up to the spring drum. If it snaps or slips off the drum, the door goes off-balance immediately, the opener detects the resistance, and stops. Garage door cable repair is also a same-day job. Delaying this repair puts extra stress on every other component in the system.

Open-Limit Settings

Every garage door opener has a setting that controls how far up the door travels before the motor stops. If this limit is set too low, the opener will cut power after only a few inches because it believes the door has already reached the top of its travel range. This can happen after a power surge, a firmware glitch, or a previous repair where the limit was inadvertently changed.

Most openers (LiftMaster, Chamberlain, Genie) have a limit adjustment dial or a pair of buttons on the motor unit. Your owner's manual will show the exact location. Increasing the open-limit by one or two increments and running the door confirms whether this was the cause. On smart openers with an app interface, limit settings may be accessible through the app.

If you are not comfortable adjusting the limits yourself, this is a quick calibration task any technician can handle in a single visit, usually included within a standard opener service call.

Why Safety Sensors Are Not the Cause When Opening

The two small sensors mounted near the base of your door tracks send an invisible beam across the opening. Most homeowners assume a sensor problem can stop a door from opening, but sensors are wired into the closing cycle only. If the beam is blocked or misaligned, the opener will refuse to close the door, or reverse it back open mid-close. They do not gate the opening cycle at all, so if your door is stopping a few inches into opening, sensors are not the cause. Check the spring, cable, limit setting, and track instead (see the sections above).

If your actual symptom is different, namely the door refusing to close, or reversing while closing, that is a sensor issue. Here's what to check in that case:

What to Look For

Indicator lights: Each sensor has a small LED. On most brands, one sensor has a steady amber (sending) light and the other has a steady green (receiving) light when everything is working. If the green receiver light is blinking or off, the sensors are not communicating.

Dirty lenses: San Diego's mild climate means garage doors stay open longer, and dust and spider webs accumulate on the sensor lenses faster than in sealed garages elsewhere. A dry cloth wipe often restores a clean signal.

Misalignment: A sensor that has been bumped, even slightly, can break the beam. Loosen the wing nut, tilt the sensor until both LEDs show steady, and retighten. This is the most common fix in homes with active pets or children.

Direct sunlight interference: This affects the closing cycle specifically. Low-angle sun shining directly into a receiver's lens can overpower the infrared beam and make the opener think it's blocked, so the door won't close (or reverses) at that time of day. The direction depends on your garage's orientation: west-facing garages are more exposed to this in the late afternoon, east-facing garages in the morning. Shielding the receiver with a small tube of cardboard or repositioning it slightly usually solves it.

Damaged wiring: The thin wires running from the sensors to the motor unit can be pinched by the door, chewed by pests, or corroded near the floor in coastal garages where salt air and moisture accelerate oxidation. A visible break or frayed spot in the wire requires replacement.

If your door is stopping while trying to open, the sensors are not the cause regardless of what their LEDs show. See the causes above instead.

Track Obstructions and Roller Damage

If the spring, cable, and limit setting all check out, walk along both sides of the door and inspect the tracks and rollers carefully. Common issues include:

  • Debris in the track: Small rocks, dirt buildup, or a bolt that has worked loose can block a roller and stop the door. Clear the track and wipe it down with a rag.
  • A cracked or flat-spotted roller: Nylon rollers are common in San Diego homes and they wear down over time, especially in garages that trap summer heat. A broken roller catches on the track edge and triggers the force protection to shut down the opener.
  • A kinked or dented track section: If the door has been hit, or a roller has jumped the track previously, the track itself may have a bend that stops the door at the same point every time.

Minor debris is an easy fix. A broken roller or a bent track section requires replacement. If the door has come partially off its tracks, do not force it. Off-track door repair involves safely guiding the door back into alignment and inspecting for secondary damage. Forcing a door along a damaged track can bend the track further and turn a small repair into a larger one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my garage door only open a few inches then stop?

The most common cause is a broken or weakening torsion spring: the opener's motor can't lift the door's full weight, so its built-in force sensor stops the door after a few inches to protect the motor and prevent injury. Other common causes are an open-limit setting that is too low, a track obstruction, or a damaged roller. Safety sensors do not stop a door from opening. They only prevent or reverse closing, so a blocked or misaligned sensor is not a cause of this specific symptom.

Can I adjust the open-limit setting on my garage door opener myself?

Yes, most openers have a limit adjustment dial or button on the motor unit. Check your owner's manual for the exact location. However, if the door still stops after adjusting the limit, or if you suspect a broken spring or cable, stop using the door and call a technician. Spring repairs involve high tension and are not safe for DIY.

Is it safe to keep using a garage door that stops partway open?

No. If the door stops after a few inches, there is likely an underlying issue such as a broken spring or worn roller. Forcing the door open manually or bypassing the opener can cause the door to drop suddenly, which is a serious safety hazard. Disconnect the opener and call a technician before using the door again.
